Dragon Chinese Zodiac: Dragon Years 1928–2024, Exact Start Dates, Earth Element & Matches

Dragon years 1928–2024 with exact Lunar New Year start dates, the Dragon's fixed yang Earth element, its Rat and Monkey trine, Rooster pairing and Dog clash.

The Dragon, 龍 lóng, is the fifth sign and the only mythical creature in the set. Its branch 辰 (chén) marks the 7 a.m. to 9 a.m. double hour. The fixed element is Earth, yang. Scholars have even proposed that the branch character once pictured a scorpion and may have stood for the star Antares.

Dragon years with exact start and end dates

The 2024 Dragon year began on February 10, so February 5, 2024 still falls in the Water Rabbit year. In 1928 and 2012 the start was January 23; in 1988 it was February 17, meaning a February 14, 1988 birthday belongs to the Fire Rabbit.[1]

Dragon compatibility in the classical system

The Dragon joins the Rat and the Monkey in the Water trine (申子辰). Its one-to-one Liu He partner is the Rooster (辰酉). The Dog sits opposite and forms its six-clash (辰戌).

Dragon in folklore and culture

In the British Library's telling of the race, the Jade Emperor is surprised that the mighty Dragon, whose great wings should have carried it to an easy win, comes in only fifth. The Dragon explains that it stopped to blow a floundering rabbit, clinging to a log, to the shore with one mighty breath.[2] Hong Kong's Education Bureau gives schoolchildren the same episode: the Dragon helps the Rabbit ashore, so the Rabbit finishes fourth and the Dragon fifth.[3]

Chinese folklore treats a Dragon year as a lucky time to have a baby, and demographers have measured the effect. Daniel Goodkind found that noticeable Dragon-year birth surges began only in 1976; his 1991 paper reports them among Chinese populations in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore and Malaysia in 1976 and 1988, but not in mainland China.[4] A 2002 study of Hong Kong links the spikes in its fertility rate in 1988 and 2000, years that went against a long downward trend, to the same preference for the Dragon.[5]

The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a zodiac figure of a dragon in Jingdezhen porcelain, painted in enamels on the biscuit, from the Kangxi period of the Qing dynasty (1662–1722).[6]

Dragon-year birth spikes are a demographic pattern driven by belief, not evidence that the sign itself brings luck.
